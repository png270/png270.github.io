It was a brief discussion with my friends during my undergrad days when they were debating the specialization they’d want to choose for their senior year. Fast forward to a few years from then, I was interning at IBM as a Security Analyst. I was blown away by the kind of work they showed us they were doing. Big tools, big names in the security industry as partners, clients and projects was all too overwhelming for me to choose a specialization within security. It was a client-facing technical consulting role within security which made me realize how much I liked brainstorming a solution as well as improving a repetitive process until it almost reaches a state where you have nothing to pick apart. The best part about all of this is how you can think of so many ways things could go wrong and what you could do to prevent it. But the fact is, it never goes wrong the way you expected it to. It was always something new, something you had to brainstorm with your team and realize “oops we didn’t make an SOP for this!”. I enjoyed this process a lot and thought I was built for this environment where you had to figure things out, design a system from scratch and iterate over it to perfect it. Until one day an incident call made me question if I knew enough to deal with the leadership team who sat there with years of experience on their hands. Hard Reset

I decided I needed to get better at what I do, study more, build more and hence, I left everything to become a master of all things security. My curiosity made me travel, it brought me to a place where I competed, built and also made me more confident about the things I could do. Penn State was that chapter. I was challenged on everything I knew from day one. I couldn’t get buffer overflow exploits right. MITM exploitation was also a challenge of its own. From desperately studying Computer Security, to diving into AI security, I with some more efforts finally learnt to overflow buffers in C, perform manual exploitation of web based vulnerabilities and learnt about backdoor attacks and model poisoning. I also had the chance to meet wonderful people at BSides NYC as well as during CTF competitions that I participated in.

During the summer between my graduate semesters, I got a chance to explore how cybersecurity interacts within the maritime industry. As an only intern, I had an amazing time working with the IT team to understand networking onboard vessels, OT architecture constraints in the industry and incident response process that defines overall safety of the crew and the cargo onboard. I remember doing a deep dive on the NotPetya incident while drafting a proposal to improve IR processes. Talking to the team at BSM, I found some really interesting problems I could solve with automation. I build a parser to detect firewall policy changes to reduce errors caused by upgrades. This felt like an extension of work I did at IBM packaged into a proper internal tool. While learning about how incidents are resolved that occur in the middle of the pacific ocean with no one around to help, I decided to build a proof-of-concept using Ansible that would keep running PowerShell scripts at regular intervals and give updates on system baselines. If there’s any deviation, the team would get an email alert to triage before it becomes an incident. I loved how cybersecurity in the maritime industry keeps you on your feet while you’re also constrained with the amount of tech you can deploy due to poor connectivity. Sometimes, it’s all about simplicity.

At this point, I feel like I have my fundamentals right but the curiosity still hasn’t left me. I’ve continued experimenting with things that interest me. I’m currently learning about AI in security and trying to find problems that allow me to build. I could be anxious about how things can break right now, but the builder in me keeps building.

The Pit Stop

Over the years, while I have learnt a lot, some observations I made haven’t left me and I’ve grown to value them personally and professionally -

  • Visibility is everything. If there’s one thing incident response has taught me, is that you can’t investigate what you can’t see. Missing logs, incomplete telemetry and unclear timestamps make it extremely difficult to debug what went wrong. I’ve started to apply this principle to what I build too. Whether it's a security tool or an AI agent, I want to understand what it's doing, why it's doing it, and when something goes wrong. Interestingly, this applies to real life as well. You need to put yourself out there to be discovered. There’s gatekeeping and then there’s hiding. You need to choose wisely.
  • Talking it out makes it make sense. When in doubt about what to choose or how to go about accomplishing something, it is always a good idea to talk it out with someone. Some of my best ideas have come from conversations I had with people around. This also made me realize that if you’re confused about what tech stack you should work with, it's quite helpful to talk to your colleague or simply an LLM. It’s because when you start putting what you’re thinking into words, things become clearer. It’s communication 101, it helps you identify where you’re lacking and why you need an alternate POV.
  • Knowing what to build, when and why. You’re often going to come across problems you’d want to solve. Some problems, however, don't need to be solved. I know it’s counter-intuitive. But I think knowing what to build because a problem getting solved can produce results is better than building something just because everyone is. Creativity in your work is more important than replicating what already exists. But it’s not a bad idea if you can take something up and make it 100x better because you found where the bottlenecks are. Fight me on this because I’m still figuring this out.

I want to become the kind of engineer who obsesses over their craft, keeps doing it over and over, in many different ways until they land on something so perfect yet it is full of flaws in their own eyes. I want to understand what works and what breaks, I want to know how things can go wrong and create something which (may or) may not wither with time.
